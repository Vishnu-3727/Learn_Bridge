The Water Cycle

The water on Earth is never used up. It moves in a continuous loop between the oceans, the sky and the land. This loop is called the water cycle.

Evaporation

The sun heats the surface of oceans, lakes and rivers. Some of that water turns into an invisible gas called water vapour and rises into the air. This change from liquid to gas is called evaporation. Plants add to it too, releasing vapour from their leaves in a process called transpiration.

Condensation

High in the atmosphere the air is much colder. As water vapour rises and cools, it turns back into tiny droplets of liquid water. This is condensation. Millions of these droplets together form a cloud.

Precipitation

When the droplets in a cloud join together they grow heavier, until the air can no longer hold them up. They then fall as rain. If the air is cold enough, they fall as snow or hail instead. Any water falling from a cloud is called precipitation.

Collection

Water that reaches the ground takes one of three paths. Some flows across the surface into streams and rivers, and eventually back to the sea. Some soaks into the soil and is taken up by plant roots. The rest seeps deeper and collects underground as groundwater, which is what a well or a borehole draws from.

Then the sun heats the surface again, and the cycle repeats.

Why it matters

The water cycle is the reason fresh water exists on land at all. Sea water is salty and cannot be drunk, but evaporation leaves the salt behind, so rain is fresh. In this way the cycle acts as a natural purifier, powered entirely by the sun.

The cycle also explains why rainfall is uneven. Monsoon winds carry moisture from the ocean over land, so coastal regions usually receive more rain than places far inland. Mountains force moist air upward, where it cools and condenses, which is why one side of a mountain range is often green while the other side lies in a dry rain shadow.

Human effects

People change the water cycle in several ways. Cutting down forests reduces transpiration, so less moisture returns to the air and the local climate can become drier. Covering ground with concrete stops rain from soaking in, so more of it runs off at once, which causes floods in cities. Drawing groundwater faster than rain can replace it makes wells run dry.

A warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour. This is why a heating planet brings both heavier downpours in some regions and longer droughts in others.
